## Single-source rows

- Row 2: Bonjour Paris (https://bonjourparis.com/history/how-a-con-man-sold-the-eiffel-tower/) confirms the rusting and the costly repairs and upkeep but does not tie repainting to the cost (only Wikipedia does), so it was not added (see below).
- Row 8: the only other source found with $20,000 + $50,000, the "Holy Cow! History" column (https://insidesources.com/holy-cow-history-the-man-who-sold-the-eiffel-tower/), retells Smithsonian point by point and gets the death year wrong (1949, not 1947), so the two count as one (see below).
- Row 12: La Libre (added above) is the only checked source that backs it: the row's first source, strangledhistory.com, says the opposite (see below).

## Conflicts to watch

Flagged only: the voice is recorded, so the video's wording stays as it is.

- Row 2 (b03 "The tower is rusting, and repainting it costs a fortune"): Smithsonian (high cost of maintenance and
  repairs), the official site (expensive maintenance) and Bonjour Paris ("in need of expensive repairs and
  maintenance") put the cost on repairs and upkeep; only Wikipedia says the city found the tower expensive to maintain
  and repaint. Bonjour Paris mentions paint only later, when workmen at the tower assess it for paint and repairs. The
  on-screen headline TOWER NEEDS COSTLY REPAIRS matches the sources. A source naming repainting as the cost would clear
  this flag.
- Row 4 (b05 "He books a suite at the Hôtel de Crillon"): History Facts, Smithsonian, Slate.fr and Bonjour Paris put
  the meeting at the Crillon. Smithsonian and Slate.fr say a room there and Mental Floss a suite in a fancy hotel, so
  "suite" is the rarer detail, not a contradiction. Wikipedia (the January 2024 revision and the current text) says only
  "an expensive hotel", so the row's Wikipedia citation no longer backs the Crillon.
- Row 5 (b05 "invites five scrap dealers"): Slate.fr, Bonjour Paris and Mental Floss say five; La Libre (2020) and the
  "Holy Cow! History" column say six; the current Wikipedia text says only "a small group", so the row's Wikipedia
  citation no longer backs the number. Five is the majority figure. Researcher-reported, not independently verified:
  Parade credits its "five" to Mental Floss, so those two count as one.
- Row 8 (b10 "So Poisson pays $20,000, plus $50,000 more to win"; the on-screen counter also runs in dollars, to 20,000
  and then to 70,000 "+ THE BRIBE"): Smithsonian is the only independent source found with this split, and the "Holy
  Cow! History" column that copies it says "the equivalent of $20,000", so it does not claim dollars. The other
  independent sources differ: the official Eiffel Tower site says "an unknown sum", and Slate.fr and History Facts say
  70,000 francs (Smithsonian's two payments also total 70,000, but in dollars). Researcher-reported, not independently
  verified: The Hustle (row 3) gives a 1.2 million franc contract plus a 70,000 franc bribe, and current Wikipedia about
  70,000 francs. If challenged, cite Smithsonian as one account; a re-voice would hedge ("by one account") or use the
  franc figure.
- Row 12 (b15 "But historians have never found a single police report. The only proof might be a con man's word"; on
  screen POLICE REPORTS: 0): La Libre is softer than the line. One writer, Jean-François Migniac (not called a
  historian), said on the TV show "Visites privées" that the Archives nationales hold no source on the sale or the
  people in it: no trace in the newspapers, none of André Poisson. Police files are not mentioned, and La Libre does not
  say the story is Lustig's own, only that the episode has often been romanticised and may be partly invented. "No one
  has ever found" would match it more closely than "historians". The row's first source, strangledhistory.com, says the
  opposite ("the paper trail is solid"), so despite its "(skeptic summary)" label it does not back this row.
  Researcher-reported, not independently verified: RetroNews (the BnF's press archive, Arnaud Pagès, 16 Sep 2020) says
  the press of the time did not report the scam, which came out only years after Lustig's death; sinistercrimes.com
  (/victor-lustig/) says no French police report or court file has been found and the story was first printed in 1961.
