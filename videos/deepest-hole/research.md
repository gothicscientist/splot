## Conflicts to watch

Flagged only: the voice is recorded, so the video's wording stays as it is.

- Row 2 (b03 "23 centimeters wide, about the size of a dinner plate"): Britannica's 9 in is 22.9 cm and EarthDate gives
  9 in (23 cm), the widely published rounded figure. No source compares the hole to a dinner plate: that is the video's
  analogy. Nautilus calls the roughly 9-inch cap over the hole dinner-plate-sized, but typical plates are 25-30 cm, so it
  is fair but loose. Researcher-reported, not independently verified: Russian technical sources give a narrower hole
  (214 mm drill head, Lobanov et al., Neftegaz.RU; open shaft today 20 cm wide to 4 km, then 10 cm, Skuf'in 2021).
- Rows 3 and 8 (b04 and b11 "nineteen years"): true only if the 12,262 m depth was reached in 1989. EarthDate and
  Wikipedia say 1989, but EarthDate and Britannica also round the project to 20 years. Researcher-reported, not
  independently verified: the Kola Science Centre paper (Skuf'in, NEXO 2021) and Lobanov et al. (Neftegaz.RU) date the
  record to 1990 (twenty years), Wikipedia also logs a breakdown at 12,262 m in June 1990, Scientific American says about
  20 years, and Guinness still lists 12,261 m by 1983 (outdated). Apart from that Guinness entry, no source disputes the
  depth or the record. If challenged, cite EarthDate and Wikipedia for 1989.
- Row 4 (b07 "The rock was 180 degrees Celsius"): EarthDate gives 180 °C at total depth in 1989 against an expected
  100 °C; Nautilus gives nearly 356 °F (180 °C), almost twice the prediction. Researcher-reported, not independently
  verified: Skuf'in (2021) and Lobanov et al. put 180 °C at about 10 km and about 212 °C at about 12 km against an
  expected 120 °C (Skuf'in elsewhere: up to 220 °C), and Guinness gives about 210 °C at the bottom. So 180 °C is the
  conservative, widely cited figure and may understate the very bottom; "far hotter than anyone expected" holds in every
  source.
